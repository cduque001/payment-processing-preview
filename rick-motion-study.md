# Rick: animation, visual style and rhythm

Study for the payment-processing opening, 24 October 2026. Scope: three relative-reach outliers in our saved corpus; all wide contact sheets and the first twenty seconds at one-second intervals inspected. Existing source records, transcripts, measured timings and earlier analyses cross-checked. This is a visual study, not an audit of the technical claims in those videos. No reference footage, artwork, mascot or narration is copied into our video.

## 1. Reverse engineering: an object the viewer wants to inspect

**0–2 seconds.** Recognizable game action is already happening inside a textured CRT. The topic sign names something concrete the viewer can see; the sign itself is not the whole hook. The housing fills the frame, so there is an immediate visual subject.

**2–13 seconds.** The same machine changes from gameplay to code, highlighted fragments, bytes and binary. The housing preserves orientation while its contents explain the change. At roughly 7–9 seconds an emphatic verdict briefly interrupts the normal motion. The prop has seams, worn paint, fitted keys, cables and shadows; the charm lives in those surfaces rather than in extra interface panels.

**14–19 seconds.** Directional signs provide a larger visual reset and explain reversal. This is a change of explanatory space, not an arbitrary transition at the end of every sentence.

**34–50 seconds.** The camera moves close when the viewer must inspect data. Rows are isolated and reorganized into a map. The movement is doing reasoning: unstructured bytes become recognizable information.

**54–66 seconds.** Gameplay and a memory scanner occupy the same frame. Damage changes the health value; subsequent scans eliminate alternatives. Input and consequence remain visible together. This is the most useful pattern for our approval-versus-payout distinction.

**Rhythm.** The stored transcript is about 3.61 words/second. Measured soft transitions have a median interval of 3.9 seconds, but there are longer continuous shots with internal changes. That figure is not an instruction to cut every four seconds. The repeated pattern is establish → inspect → change → see the consequence. The late history/tools detour becomes denser and is less useful to our short opening.

**Transfer.** Keep a recognizable payment reader while its display changes. Give the viewer a cup and a price before naming financial roles. Reveal the shop's separate payout status alongside approval. Push into the request only when the narration asks what the message means.

## 2. HTTP: behavior makes the comparison

**0–5 seconds.** A browser window populates while the protocol variants accumulate above it. Changes in the interface accompany the question. This opening requires more pre-existing technical interest than a physical purchase, so we borrow its motion logic rather than its information density.

**6–22 seconds.** The browser and server establish two stable endpoints. Files appear between them. Retaining those endpoints lets the viewer learn the stage once.

**36–46 seconds.** A large image occupies the connection while smaller files wait. The cost is visible before the term is emphasized. The waiting objects do explanatory work; random ambient particles would not.

**58–70 seconds.** Smaller pieces share a connection, and the browser fills as pieces arrive. The output stays visible. At **80–90 seconds**, a closer view isolates the lost packet and the blocked traffic. At **106–112 seconds**, independent streams keep moving around one lost item. The difference is conveyed by changed behavior with comparable positions.

**Style.** Restrained amber/blue/green roles, off-white retro equipment, dark dotted ground, old desktop chrome. The dots and screen glare are atmosphere; the readable route is the lesson. The original small stage, tiny counters and mascot column are unsuitable for our full-screen phone layout.

**Rhythm.** Roughly 3.11 words/second. The transition detector records zero major soft transitions; the sheets nevertheless show file travel, queues, state changes and camera moves. A cut-count metric alone would misdiagnose this as static. Keep a camera still when the request itself supplies the action.

**Transfer.** Blue always means payment information. Build the route as the voice names the processor, network and issuing bank. Do not animate money leaving the shop or arriving in its account during authorization. The main request has one identity, one amount and one destination.

## 3. Load balancers: a persistent system with changing behavior

**0–10 seconds.** Incoming requests accumulate in one backend until capacity becomes a visible problem. The mechanism is immediately present, although the strongest question arrives late. We can improve the opening by foregrounding the surprising outcome: approval appears before the shop has necessarily been paid.

**12–30 seconds.** More servers appear, creating the need for routing. A selector is introduced only after that need exists. The same objects stay on the stage.

**34–58 seconds.** A rotating arm demonstrates different routing policies. Motion is the rule, not a decoration over a spoken definition. At **68–80 seconds**, probe/reply traffic changes the set of destinations. At **98–110 seconds**, the same session identity survives the trip and exposes the wrong destination.

**Rhythm.** Roughly 3.62 words/second. Only two soft transitions were detected because most of the short retains one set. Tokens, selector motion and live changes sustain it. The grey treatment becomes repetitive later; constant motion is insufficient if successive states look too similar.

**Transfer.** Follow one $5 request. Show each handoff with a purposeful accelerate–travel–land gesture, then allow the destination to remain readable. Build a fresh visual event roughly every 1–3 seconds in our opening, but let the words determine when it happens. Avoid looping unrelated traffic that could look like repeated charges.

## The payment build

The existing narration remains unchanged: 86 words across the first two beats, approximately 25.4 seconds with the inter-beat pause. Its average is about 3.4 words/second, already near the studied range. Faster speech is not necessary to make the scenes feel alive.

**A — The café receipt.** Ivory paper, detailed sage/cream illustrated reader, tactile cup, strong black type and blue request markings. The purchase becomes a long receipt-like route; one amount moves through it. This is the warmer, more characterful direction.

**B — Inside the tap.** Graphite product imagery, cyan information paths, controlled green approval, a close camera move into a request tunnel. The route is revealed in depth and then resolves into an account inspection. This is the more cinematic direction.

Both begin with objects in motion, land the physical tap on the word “tap,” show approval at “approved,” and reveal the separate payout state on “But.” Each has its own transition into scene two and maintains continuity across that boundary. Captions occupy a separate safe band. Both use identical audio, opening impact and restrained cues, so the comparison is about animation and style.

The intended hook is visual: **a successful tap does not mean every step is finished.** The audience sees the consequence before meeting the processor, card network or issuing bank. No introductory equation, logo montage or heading-only opening is needed.

## Review criteria

At phone size, a new viewer should be able to identify the coffee purchase without reading captions, distinguish approval from payout, follow the blue request in the correct order, and recognize that the final bank is the issuer that can inspect the cardholder's account. The first and second scenes must meet without a reset. Test a full playback, both styles, seek/restart, audio continuity and a muted inspection of the main animation.
